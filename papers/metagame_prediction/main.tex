\title{Predicting Human Metagame Adaptation to Expansions and Balance
Patches in a Collectible Card Game}

\maketitle

\begin{abstract}
Expansions and balance patches change which cards a population of
players puts in its decks. We test whether this response can be
predicted from simulation before it happens. The inputs are a
card-accurate simulator of 2014-era \emph{Hearthstone} and the real
decks played before each of two historical shocks, the Naxxramas
expansion (July 2014) and the Starving Buzzard nerf (September 2014).
Ground truth for the outcome comes from 33{,}697 ranked decklists.
Unbiased evolutionary deck search adopts new cards at roughly the
mutation-drift rate, because a single card changes a 30-card deck's
win rate by about 1$\pp$ while affordable per-deck evaluation is an
order of magnitude noisier. We instead measure each candidate card's
marginal win-rate contribution in fixed real host decks. This probe
recovers the community's adoption ranking of the new set. Evolution
whose mutation operator is weighted by probed values, a stand-in for
players' deliberate testing of promising cards, then reproduces the
post-shock decklists on the cards that moved. Predictions are
three-seed ensembles with a calibrated readout. Direction hit-rate on
Hunter cards whose real adoption shifted rises from 16/38 under
unbiased evolution to 31/38, and the nerf experiment predicts the
Buzzard collapse (predicted within-class adoption 73\% to 12\%,
against a real fall to 17\%) while cutting the un-nerfed survivors
too deep. A rolling variant separates one-step skill from forecast
horizon: re-anchored single-period predictions reach Spearman
0.54--0.84 on moved cards and track the Buzzard collapse period by
period, while a free-running forecast locks into a self-play echo
chamber and misses the nerf response entirely. Two cards are mispredicted in
every configuration. Both misses trace to the 1-ply piloting agent,
and a learned evaluator with an identity-free card encoding
partially closes them. Simulated meta
prediction has previously been validated against real usage shifts
for Pok\'emon tier bans; we provide the corresponding validation for
a collectible card game, covering shock types that a ban cannot
express: new content and stat changes.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}

Live competitive games are periodically shocked. Expansions inject
new content, and balance patches weaken cards that dominate. Within
weeks of either event the distribution of played decks shifts as
players work out what the change is worth. Designers would like to
anticipate that shift before shipping, and academic work on automated
balancing has long evaluated proposed changes inside a simulator.
Until recently, no study checked the simulated response against what
a real player population went on to do.

We pose the forward-prediction problem directly. Fix a historical
shock in \emph{Hearthstone}: the Naxxramas expansion (July~22, 2014,
30 new cards) or the Starving Buzzard/Leeroy Jenkins nerf
(September~22, 2014). Give the system a card-accurate simulator and
the real decks played before the shock. Ask it to predict the decks
played after: which cards get adopted, which get dropped, at what
rates. Ground truth is measured from an archive of 33{,}697 ranked
2014 decklists. Saravanan and Guzdial~\cite{saravanan2024meta}
recently established this validate-against-the-real-population
standard for Pok\'emon tier bans. We extend it to a deckbuilding
game, and to two shock types a ban cannot express: new content, for
which no historical usage data exists, and stat changes, where the
modified card remains legal at a different power level.

Our contributions:

\begin{itemize}
\item A negative result with a quantified mechanism. Unbiased
evolutionary deck search cannot detect single-card quality at
feasible budgets. A one-card swap moves a deck's win rate by
$\sim$1$\pp$, well under the noise of affordable per-deck evaluation,
and measured adoption of new cards under unbiased evolution matches
the pure mutation-drift rate.
\item A marginal-value probe that isolates the card variable by
swapping each candidate into a fixed slot of real host decks and
measuring the win-rate delta over fixed opposition. The probe
recovers the real community's adoption ranking of the Naxxramas set,
and its two systematic misses localise the piloting agent's skill
ceiling, which we quantify independently.
\item Probe-biased evolution, in which exploration is weighted by
probed card values to stand in for players' deliberate testing of
promising cards. It reproduces real post-shock adoption on the cards
that moved, for the expansion and for the nerf. A no-change baseline
wins overall rank correlation because most cards never move; the
prediction wins on the movers. We report both.
\item A dissociation in the real data with a matching simulated
account. The nerf collapsed Buzzard's within-class adoption from
68\% to 17\% while Hunter's class share stayed flat, so players
abandoned the card and kept the class. A replicator-style analysis
shows why: month-to-month archetype shares in this archive sit at
their sampling noise floor, whereas card adoption around shocks
moves by 40--60$\pp$.
\item A learned value function whose card encoding is identity-free
(mechanics, statistics and keywords, with no card IDs). It
generalises to cards it has never seen when its training data covers
the mechanism involved, and it assigns the correct sign to both 2014
nerfs given the patch text alone.
\end{itemize}

\section{Related Work}

Hearthstone has served as an AI testbed for a decade, mostly for
playing strength or for deckbuilding within a fixed metagame.
Garc\'ia-S\'anchez et al.~\cite{garcia2016evodeck} established
evolutionary deckbuilding. Fontaine et
al.~\cite{fontaine2019mapelites} mapped deck spaces with
quality-diversity search, and the Hearthstone AI
competition~\cite{dockhorn2019competition} drove play agents,
including MCTS with learned components~\cite{swiechowski2018}.
Justesen et al.~\cite{justesen2017} showed that in multi-action
adversarial games the effective unit of decision is the turn plan, a
result we use when analysing our piloting agent's limits. Dockhorn
et al.~\cite{dockhorn2018prediction} studied prediction of hidden
information in Hearthstone; since they found exact hand knowledge to
be worth only a few points of win rate, we let our agents pilot with
perfect information.

The closest prior work comes in two steps. de Mesentier Silva et
al.~\cite{demesentier2019meta} evolved decks to study how proposed
card changes would shift a simulated metagame, with the metagame
internal to the simulator. Saravanan and
Guzdial~\cite{saravanan2024meta} then added the validation their
setting lacked: the Meta Discovery framework uses
reinforcement-learning agents in Pok\'emon Showdown to predict the
post-ban top-40 usage meta for four real tier bans, checked against
Smogon's measured usage statistics, where it reaches 92--100\%
overlap. We adopt the same validation standard. Our setting differs
in the domain (a deckbuilding game, where the predicted unit is
within-deck card adoption over a pool rather than pick rates over a
fixed roster), in the shock types (content addition and a stat
nerf), and in the explicit no-change baseline that our sticky ground
truth makes necessary. Community analytics such as the Vicious
Syndicate Data Reaper reports~\cite{vicious_syndicate} and the
HearthPwn deck archive~\cite{zenodo_dataset} supply ground truth.
CMA-ES~\cite{hansen2001cmaes} and
MAP-Elites~\cite{mouret2015mapelites} supply optimisation machinery.

\section{The Simulator and Its Validation}
\label{sec:simulator}

\subsection{Engine and scope}
The simulator implements \emph{Hearthstone}'s 2014-era Classic
format restricted to three classes (Hunter, Mage, Warrior): 221
Classic cards plus all 24 Naxxramas cards those classes could play,
245 in total. It models the full turn structure, combat, secrets,
weapons, auras, deathrattles and triggered effects. A systematic
audit compared every card against the authoritative card text of the
era and left each with a regression test. A patching hook applies
historical balance changes, so the September 2014 patch (Starving
Buzzard 2-mana 2/1 to 5-mana 3/2; Leeroy Jenkins 4 to 5 mana) can be
switched on and off. All 88 archived real Classic decklists in our
evaluation snapshot are exactly constructible in the pool.

\subsection{Matchup fidelity and the agent-skill ceiling}
\label{sec:fidelity}
We validated the engine by asking whether simulated matchups between
real decks reproduce real matchup outcomes. Ground truth is a
community-measured Classic matchup matrix built from roughly 90k
tracked games~\cite{vicious_syndicate}, which covers 20 in-scope
archetype pairings with banded win rates and exact ordinal ranks.
With a tuned 1-ply greedy agent (a 27-feature linear evaluation)
piloting both sides, the Spearman rank correlation between simulated
and real matchup orderings is 0.33. With guided MCTS over the same
evaluation it is 0.55--0.61. Since the evaluator is unchanged, the
gain comes from deeper search, and it concentrates in the archetype
whose real gameplan requires valuing delayed wins (Freeze Mage).
Residual fidelity error is therefore dominated by agent skill; we
track this confound through the rest of the paper. Consistent with
that reading, a full-pool card audit that changed game content
(including a token-trigger fix without which the combo the 2014 nerf
targeted did not exist in-engine) moved aggregate fidelity by less
than sampling noise. An 82-deck round-robin against per-deck real
win rates gives Spearman 0.60.

\section{Historical Data and Ground Truth}
\label{sec:data}

\subsection{The deck archive and its date trap}
Ground truth derives from 33{,}697 ranked 2014 decklists (HearthPwn
archive~\cite{zenodo_dataset}), normalised to the simulator's
three-class world. One correction matters for anyone using this
archive. Deck dates are creation dates, but card lists reflect the
last edit, so a deck created in June and edited in August can
contain Naxxramas cards ``before'' Naxxramas existed. Filtering on
the expansion tag of each deck's cards removed 3{,}725 such decks
from the pre-Naxxramas window. Without the filter, the prediction
task is contaminated by its own answer key.

\subsection{Two shocks, and a dissociation}
\label{sec:shocks}
Table~\ref{tab:buzzard_series} shows the monthly series around the
September nerf, and Fig.~\ref{fig:dissociation} the same ground
truth re-cut into periods bounded by the two patch dates (a calendar
September mixes 21 pre-nerf days with 8 post-nerf days; the
period-resolved Buzzard fall is 52.8\% to 16.2\% across the
boundary). The monthly and period series also differ slightly in
level because the period series applies the Set-tag filter of
Sec.~IV-A, which the monthly aggregation predates. At the card
level,
Starving Buzzard's within-Hunter adoption falls from a 67.7\%
pre-nerf mean to 16.9\% afterwards, beginning in the nerf month
itself. At the class level, Hunter's share of submitted decks is
flat to slightly rising over the same period (31.6\% to 33.0\%).
The popular claim that the nerf killed Hunter finds no support in
this archive; players dropped the card and rebuilt the class around
other packages. A simulator that reproduces the card-level collapse
is capturing card power. One that also predicts what replaces it is
capturing adaptation.

\subsection{Why shocks are the predictable part}
\label{sec:dynamics}
Before predicting shocks we tested the smoother alternative: does an
archetype's field-weighted simulated win rate (its matchup row
weighted by the current popularity mix) predict its next-month
popularity change, in the manner of replicator dynamics? Over 36
(archetype, month) points in the pre-Naxxramas window the Spearman
correlation is $-0.004$, and $0.081$ when the matchup matrix is
re-simulated with the stronger MCTS agent, so the null does not
depend on the choice of agent. The target itself appears to be
noise at this granularity. At $\sim$580 classified decks per month,
the binomial standard error of a share change ($\sim$2.2$\pp$) is
comparable to the observed mean absolute change (2.9$\pp$), and the
lag-1 autocorrelation of changes ($-0.46$) sits at the $-0.5$
expected for a constant series plus independent noise. In a
separate 88-deck snapshot, per-deck win rate and play count are
uncorrelated (Spearman $-0.03$). Card adoption around shocks moves
by 40--60$\pp$ against that $\sim$2$\pp$ floor, so the remaining
experiments target card adoption.

\section{Prediction Pipeline}
\label{sec:pipeline}

Fig.~\ref{fig:pipeline} summarises the pipeline. All experiments
share the same skeleton: seed with the real pre-shock decks, evolve
under the post-shock card pool (expansion cards added, or nerf
patches applied), extract per-card adoption from the evolved
population, and compare against real post-shock adoption. Deck
fitness is evaluated by simulated matches against a refreshing
field: a gauntlet seeded with real pre-shock representatives of all
three classes and periodically re-sampled from the co-evolving
populations' elites, with a fraction of real anchors retained,
because a fixed gauntlet measurably overfits. Decks are piloted by
the tuned greedy agent with self-play-optimised weights. Every
metric is also computed for the no-change baseline, which predicts
that post-shock adoption equals pre-shock adoption.

\subsection{Attempt 1: unbiased evolution fails, with a mechanism}
Real pre-Naxxramas seeds evolving under the Classic+Naxxramas pool
adopted approximately none of the cards the real community adopted.
The failure is one of drift rather than selection. A single-card
swap changes a deck's win rate by $\sim$1$\pp$, the per-deck
evaluation budget (4--12 games under early stopping) has a noise
floor an order of magnitude above that, and the observed adoption
rate of new cards matches the pure mutation-drift expectation.
Whole-deck fitness evaluation cannot resolve single-card quality at
any budget we could afford, which for this study was roughly
37{,}000 games.

\subsection{The marginal-value probe}
The probe isolates the card variable directly. For each candidate
card and class, swap the candidate into a fixed slot of five real
host decks, play a fixed 60-game schedule per host against a fixed
nine-deck real field, and record the mean win-rate delta relative to
the unmodified hosts. The result is one number per (card, class):
the card's marginal value in a realistic deck context.

\subsection{Attempt 2: probe-biased evolution}
Real players do not explore a new set uniformly at random. They read
the cards and disproportionately test the ones that look strong. We
model this with a biased mutation operator: probed-positive cards
are proposed more often, and probed-negative slots are replaced more
often, while selection is left untouched (24 fixed games per
evaluation, 25 generations). The operator also respects the copy
structure of real decks. Real 2014 lists average 18 distinct cards
in 30 slots, running second copies of most non-legendary cards,
while an unbiased swap over the $\sim$190-card legal pool erodes
duplicates toward all-singleton decks. Mutation therefore up-weights
both doubling a held single copy and removing one, with legendaries
capped at one copy, which holds evolved decks at 19.8 distinct cards
against the real 18.0. The claim under test is that
simulation-derived card values combined with evolutionary selection
reproduce the shift. Blind evolution does not discover the shift on
its own, and we report the unbiased failure so that the two claims
stay separate.

\subsection{Readout and replication}
\label{sec:readout}
Raw elite counts overstate adoption in two ways that a rank metric
never sees: a card in all of a niche's 18--40 elites reads as
exactly 100\%, and evolved elites spread mass over slightly more
distinct cards than real decks do. The reported readout removes
both with a Jeffreys shrinkage of each per-card count followed by
one monotone logit shift that rescales the predicted total card
mass to its pre-shock real value. The shift is order-preserving, so
every ranking metric is untouched; only levels change. Each
one-shot experiment is run at three seeds, each seed's readout is
corrected as above, and the reported forecast is their mean, with
per-seed spread quoted alongside. All prediction figures and tables
use this readout.

\section{Results}
\label{sec:results}

\subsection{The probe recovers the real adoption ranking}
For the Naxxramas launch, the probe's top Hunter cards are the real
community's staple package: Sludge Belcher $+6.5\pp$, Mad Scientist
$+5.3\pp$, Webspinner $+5.1\pp$, Haunted Creeper $+3.7\pp$. All four
reached 45--74\% real adoption within weeks. The cards the community
never played probe at the bottom. Spearman correlation between
probed value and real pre-nerf adoption over the 22 new cards per
class is 0.35 (Hunter), 0.20 (Mage) and 0.30 (Warrior). Both
systematic misses have a mechanical explanation. Death's Bite
(probed last for Warrior, against 60\% real adoption) is a weapon
whose value depends on sequencing its deathrattle across turns, and
Duplicate ($-3.0\pp$ probed, 41\% real) is a secret whose value is
contextual card advantage. A 1-ply evaluator piloting a single-card
swap cannot see either property, and both cards sit under the
agent-skill ceiling measured in Sec.~\ref{sec:fidelity}.

\subsection{Expansion: predicting Naxxramas adoption}
Probe-biased evolution from real pre-Naxxramas seeds adopts the real
package. On the 38 Hunter cards whose real adoption moved by at
least 5$\pp$ across the launch, the predicted direction matches
reality for 31 (26--31 across seeds), against 16 for unbiased
evolution; chance is 19. Over the movers the ensemble reaches
Spearman 0.81 for Hunter (per-seed $0.74\pm0.02$), 0.59 for Mage
and 0.67 for Warrior, with a pooled adoption MAE of 6.2$\pp$.
Magnitudes land in range for the package: Mad Scientist is
predicted at 42.5\% within Hunter against a real 32.7\%, Sludge
Belcher, Undertaker and Loatheb all rise from zero, and Kel'Thuzad
is correctly left on the shelf (2.9\% predicted for Mage, 4.5\%
real). The clear underestimates are
Haunted Creeper (8.6\% predicted, 57.6\% real) and Webspinner
(13.4\%, 54.4\%), cheap sticky minions the probe itself ranks near
the top of the set; the bias proposes them and selection keeps
them, but nothing in the pipeline pushes a good card to the near
ubiquity these two reached. Death's Bite stays near 0\% predicted,
since a pipeline built on probed values inherits the probe's blind
spots.

\subsection{Patch: predicting the nerf response}
The nerf experiment is the cleaner test, since no new cards are
involved and the real post-shock pool equals the simulated pool.
Seeds are real Naxxramas-era decks; the pool applies the real
September patch. Table~\ref{tab:nerf} and Fig.~\ref{fig:nerf} show
the headline cards, and Fig.~\ref{fig:dissociation} places the
Buzzard prediction against the period series. Both nerfed cards
collapse to within a few points of their real landing spots:
Buzzard 12.2\% predicted against 16.8\% real (seed range
6--21\%), Leeroy 6.6\% against 12.0\%. Over the Hunter movers the
ensemble reaches Spearman 0.82 (per-seed $0.75\pm0.05$), with 0.71
for Mage and 0.72 for Warrior. The model's error sits on the
survivors rather than the targets: Unleash the Hounds, which the
patch left alone, is kept but cut too deep (46.0\% predicted,
69.5\% real, and a 24--70\% seed spread that makes it the least
stable prediction in the table), and Webspinner is held flat where
reality rose. The pipeline over-cuts inside the nerf's blast
radius while calling the nerfed cards themselves. The removal
probe alone already carries the sign of the response: deleting
Buzzard from patched-world Hunter decks gains $+2.7\pp$, deleting
Leeroy $+0.9\pp$ (Hunter) and $+1.6\pp$ (Warrior).

\subsection{The nerf moved the field, not only the decks}
\label{sec:composition}
The prediction pipeline treats within-class card adoption as the
target, but a class is a mixture of archetypes, and the Buzzard nerf
shifted that mixture. Classifying the archive Hunter decks into Face
and Midrange (Sunshine) by signature cards, Sunshine's share falls
from 57.6\% to 29.8\% across the nerf while Face rises from 40.0\% to
67.7\% ($z=5.4$, the largest composition shift in the dataset). The
Buzzard-and-Hyena beast package the patch targeted lived in Sunshine
Hunter, and when it died the field converged on Face aggro. The
shift survives two controls that rule out classifier circularity:
recomputing the mix with Buzzard removed from every archetype
signature still gives 40\% to 62\% Face, and Leper Gnome, a
Face-only marker the patch never touched, independently rises from
36\% to 59\% across the same boundary. Part of the nerf's card-level
effect is therefore recomposition of the field rather than
substitution inside a fixed deck.

This is a descriptive finding, not a modelling lever. An oracle test
that hands a mixture model the true post-shock archetype mix, the
ceiling any such model could reach, improves adoption MAE by
$0.37\pp$ over pooled prediction across 799 card-transitions,
because 90\% of real card movement is within-archetype substitution;
archetype membership explains a median 5\% of per-card inclusion
variance, and for Mage essentially none. Predicting per archetype
and mixing would not repay its cost.

\subsection{Trajectory: one-step skill, free-running drift}
\label{sec:rolling}
The one-shot experiments predict a single post-shock equilibrium. A
rolling variant predicts the whole trajectory at the archive's data
resolution: five periods bounded by the two patch dates (seed period
Apr~1--Jul~21, then four predicted periods, each with 117--673 decks
per class), stepping the same probe-biased evolution through each
period's pool state. Two seeding regimes separate one-step skill
from forecast horizon. In \emph{anchored} mode each period is seeded
from the previous period's real decks; in \emph{free-running} mode
only the seed period is real, and every later period starts from the
model's own previous elite population.

Anchored one-step predictions are strong everywhere: over cards
whose real adoption moved by at least 5$\pp$ between consecutive
periods, Spearman correlations run 0.54--0.84 for every class in
every period after the first (all $p\le0.04$), per-period adoption
MAE stays between 3.1 and 4.6$\pp$, and the predicted Buzzard
trajectory tracks the real collapse
(Fig.~\ref{fig:dissociation}), reaching 24.3\% against a real
17.5\% by year's end while starting the fall one period early. The
free-running forecast keeps significant rank correlations on
Hunter and Warrior movers through all four periods (0.53--0.68
after the first), loses Mage in the post-nerf periods, and never
drops Buzzard: 86--96\% through the post-nerf periods against a
real 16--18\%. Since the readout of Sec.~\ref{sec:readout} already
removes the finite-sample ceiling, that persistence is genuine
model behaviour, not an artifact of counting elites. The mechanism
is an echo chamber. Once every elite deck carries a card, the
evaluation gauntlet (drawn from that same population) offers no
opponent against which dropping it helps, and selection pressure to
remove the card disappears. Re-anchoring on real decks breaks the
loop. The pattern matches numerical forecasting generally: skillful
single steps, compounding drift over the horizon, and the useful
horizon of the present pipeline is one period, roughly six weeks.

\subsection{The baseline}
The no-change baseline wins the overall adoption Spearman, at
0.64--0.95 against the prediction's 0.52--0.65. Real decks are
sticky, and a baseline that copies roughly 100 unmoved cards is
unbeatable on a rank metric dominated by non-movers. The
prediction's advantage is confined to the movers, in direction
hit-rate and in the magnitudes above. Predicting the movers is the
part of the question a no-change baseline cannot answer by
construction, so we report both numbers and leave the weighting to
the reader.

\section{Learned Evaluators and Unseen Cards}
\label{sec:neural}

The probe's evaluator is a hand-designed linear function, and the
new cards were probed with full knowledge of their implementation. A
stronger test of generalisation replaces it with a learned value
function that has never seen the new cards. Each card is encoded by
68 identity-free mechanical features (cost and stats, type,
keywords, trigger and effect categories, effect magnitudes), so a
nerfed card encodes differently from its original. A small deep-sets
value network of about 60k parameters is trained on pre-Naxxramas
self-play only, with Monte Carlo outcome targets and no search in
the training loop.

Probing the Naxxramas cards with the net before it has seen them,
the per-class Spearman against real adoption is $+0.30$ (Mage),
$+0.20$ (Warrior) and $-0.075$ (Hunter), and the Hunter package,
which is deathrattle-token cards with no support in pre-Naxxramas
training data, is where it fails. These per-class correlations are
too weak to lean on: with 22 cards per class their 95\% intervals
all straddle zero except Mage after fine-tuning, and a repeat of the
identical probe condition moved the fine-tuned Hunter value from
$+0.34$ to $+0.16$ (run-to-run per-card standard deviation
$1.3\pp$, rank agreement $\rho=0.69$). We report them as directional
only.

The reproducible evidence for the agent-skill diagnosis is at the
card level, on the two cards the linear probe most badly misordered.
Death's Bite (60\% real adoption, probed last by the linear agent at
$-3.2\pp$) moves to near zero under every net-based pilot we ran:
$-0.4$, $-0.6$ and $-1.2\pp$ for the fine-tuned net, an identical
rerun, and turn-plan beam search over the net. Duplicate ($-3.0\pp$
linear) moves the same way, to $-0.4$ and $-1.3\pp$. A stronger
evaluator roughly halves the error on exactly the sequencing- and
synergy-dependent cards the 1-ply agent cannot value, which is the
mechanism the paper attributes the misses to. The misses do not
fully close, since none of these evaluators reaches the $+3$ to
$+4\pp$ a correct valuation would need, so the residual is a policy
limitation, not an evaluation one. Fine-tuning the net on Naxxramas
self-play also lifts Mage to $+0.60$, and given only the patch text
the net signs both 2014 nerfs correctly.

\section{Limitations}
\label{sec:limitations}

The probe bias injects simulation-derived knowledge into
exploration, and unbiased evolution fails; we frame the claim
accordingly. Both persistent misses trace to the piloting agent's
1-ply horizon, quantified independently by the fidelity study, so
claims about sequencing-dependent cards are out of reach at this
agent strength. The engine models three classes and one era, and
cross-class effects, such as a nerf pushing players toward an
unmodelled class, are invisible to it. The pre-nerf ground-truth
window may retain a small number of post-nerf-edited decks, because
the September patch received no archive tag of its own; the
window's healthy 73.4\% Buzzard rate bounds that contamination as
minor. Ground-truth matchup win rates are banded at $\pm2.5\pp$,
so we use ordinal ranks where precision matters. The one-shot
predictions are three-seed ensembles with the spread reported;
individual cards can still swing widely across seeds (Unleash the
Hounds spans 24--70\%), and the rolling trajectories are single
runs per mode. The probe aggregates five hosts and 60 games per
card but is itself a single run.

\section{Conclusion}

Given pre-shock decklists and a validated simulator, a
marginal-value probe combined with probe-biased evolutionary
selection predicts which cards a real player population adopted and
abandoned around an expansion and around a nerf. The failure modes
are quantified and localise to agent skill, the no-change baseline
is reported alongside the wins, and a learned evaluator shows the
same predictions emerging from card mechanics alone where training
coverage allows. Forward validation against measured human
adoption, recently established for tier
bans~\cite{saravanan2024meta}, is a standard the automated-balancing
literature can now be held to. This work extends its coverage to
the expansion and nerf shocks that make up most of a collectible
card game's life.
