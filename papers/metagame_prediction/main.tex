\title{Predicting Human Metagame Adaptation to Expansions and Balance
Patches in a Collectible Card Game}

\author{\IEEEauthorblockN{Alex Elton-Pym}
\IEEEauthorblockA{\textit{(affiliation TODO)}\\
alexeltonpym@gmail.com}}

\maketitle

\begin{abstract}
When a collectible card game receives an expansion or a balance patch,
its player population responds by rebuilding decks --- adopting some new
cards at high rates, ignoring others, and abandoning nerfed cards. We ask
whether this human response can be predicted \emph{forward} from
simulation alone: given only the real decks played before a shock, can a
validated game simulator predict which cards players will adopt and drop
afterwards? Using a card-accurate simulator of 2014-era
\emph{Hearthstone} and an archive of 33{,}697 ranked decklists spanning
the Naxxramas expansion (July 2014) and the Starving Buzzard nerf
(September 2014), we show that (i) unbiased evolutionary deck search
fails to detect single-card quality at feasible evaluation budgets ---
adoption of new cards matches the pure mutation-drift rate; (ii) a
\emph{marginal-value probe} that measures each candidate card's win-rate
contribution in fixed real host decks recovers the real adoption ranking
(the community's staple picks probe highest; never-played cards probe at
the bottom); and (iii) evolution whose exploration is biased by these
probed values --- modelling players' deliberate, non-uniform testing of
new cards --- reproduces the real post-shock decklists on the cards that
actually moved: direction hit-rate on real Hunter movers rises from
16/38 to 30/38, and the nerf experiment predicts the Starving Buzzard
collapse (real $73\%\!\to\!17\%$ within-class adoption, predicted
$73\%\!\to\!32\%$) while correctly retaining the un-nerfed cards around
it. The two persistent misses (a weapon whose value lies in multi-turn
sequencing, and a secret whose value is contextual card advantage) trace
to measured limitations of the piloting agent rather than to card data,
and are partially closed by an ID-free learned evaluator that
generalises to unseen cards from their mechanics. To our knowledge this
is the first validation of simulated metagame prediction against
measured human adoption data around real balance events.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}

Live competitive games are periodically shocked: expansions inject new
content, and balance patches weaken cards that dominate. Each shock
triggers a population-level response --- within weeks, the distribution
of played decks shifts as players test new cards, keep the good ones,
and abandon the nerfed ones. Game designers would like to anticipate
this response before shipping a change; academic work on automated
balancing has long used simulation to evaluate proposed changes
\emph{in silico}, but almost never validates the simulated response
against what a real player population subsequently did.

This paper poses the forward-prediction problem directly. Fix a
historical shock in \emph{Hearthstone}: the Naxxramas expansion
(July~22, 2014, 30 new cards) or the Starving Buzzard/Leeroy Jenkins
nerf (September~22, 2014). Give the system only (a) a card-accurate
simulator of the game and (b) the real decks played \emph{before} the
shock. Ask it to predict the real decks played \emph{after}: which cards
get adopted, which get dropped, at what rates. Ground truth is measured
from an archive of 33{,}697 ranked 2014 decklists.

The problem is harder than it looks, and the failures are as
informative as the successes. Our contributions:

\begin{itemize}
\item A negative result with a quantified mechanism: \textbf{unbiased
evolutionary deck search cannot see single-card quality} at feasible
budgets. A one-card swap moves a 30-card deck's win rate by
$\sim$1$\pp$, far below the noise of affordable per-deck evaluation, and
measured adoption of new cards under unbiased evolution matches the
pure mutation-drift rate.
\item A \textbf{marginal-value probe} that isolates the variable:
swap each candidate card into a fixed slot of real host decks and
measure the win-rate delta over fixed opposition. The probe recovers
the real community's adoption ranking of the Naxxramas set, and its
systematic misses localise exactly where the piloting agent's skill
ceiling lies.
\item \textbf{Probe-biased evolution} --- exploration weighted by
probed card values, modelling the fact that real players do not test
new cards uniformly at random --- which reproduces the real post-shock
adoption on the cards that moved, for both the expansion and the nerf,
against a strong no-change baseline that we report honestly (the
baseline wins overall rank correlation because most cards do not move;
the prediction wins on the movers, which is the question).
\item A class-vs-card dissociation in the real data with a matching
simulated account: the Buzzard nerf collapsed \emph{card} adoption
($68\%\!\to\!17\%$) while \emph{class} share stayed flat --- players
abandoned the card, not the class --- and a replicator-style analysis
shows month-to-month archetype shares carry too little signal to
predict, whereas card adoption around shocks is the predictable
quantity.
\item An extension replacing the hand-designed evaluator with an
\textbf{ID-free learned value function} that encodes cards by mechanics
rather than identity, generalises to never-seen cards where its
training distribution covers the mechanism, and signs both 2014 nerfs
from the patch text alone.
\end{itemize}

\section{Related Work}

Hearthstone has been a standard AI testbed for a decade, but almost all
work targets \emph{playing} strength or \emph{deckbuilding} in a fixed
metagame. Evolutionary deckbuilding was established by
Garc\'ia-S\'anchez et al.~\cite{garcia2016evodeck}; quality-diversity
methods mapped deck spaces~\cite{fontaine2019mapelites}; and the
Hearthstone AI competition~\cite{dockhorn2019competition} drove play
agents including MCTS with learned components~\cite{swiechowski2018}.
Closest to our motivation, de Mesentier Silva et
al.~\cite{demesentier2019meta} evolved decks to study how proposed
balance changes would shift a simulated metagame --- the key difference
is validation: their metagame is internal to the simulator, whereas we
test predictions against the measured response of the real 2014 player
population. Justesen et al.~\cite{justesen2017} showed that in
multi-action adversarial games the effective unit of decision is the
turn plan rather than the atomic action, which informs our analysis of
the piloting agent's limitations. Dockhorn et
al.~\cite{dockhorn2018prediction} studied prediction of hidden
information in Hearthstone; we use their finding on the limited value
of exact hand knowledge to justify our perfect-information piloting.
On the human side, large-scale deck archives have been mined
descriptively~\cite{zenodo_dataset}, and community analytics such as
the Vicious Syndicate Data Reaper reports~\cite{vicious_syndicate}
publish matchup matrices from tens of thousands of tracked games; we
use both as ground truth rather than as objects of study. CMA-ES
\cite{hansen2001cmaes} and MAP-Elites~\cite{mouret2015mapelites}
supply the optimisation machinery.

\section{The Simulator and Its Validation}
\label{sec:simulator}

\subsection{Engine and scope}
We implement a card-accurate simulator of \emph{Hearthstone}'s
2014-era Classic format restricted to three classes (Hunter, Mage,
Warrior): 221 Classic cards plus all 24 Naxxramas cards choosable by
those classes (21 neutrals and one card per class), 245 total. The
engine models the full turn structure, combat, secrets, weapons,
auras, deathrattles and triggered effects; a systematic audit compared
every card against the authoritative card text of the era and added a
per-card regression test suite. A patching hook applies historical
balance changes (the September 2014 patch: Starving Buzzard 2-mana
2/1$\to$5-mana 3/2; Leeroy Jenkins 4$\to$5 mana), so pre- and
post-patch worlds are both simulable. Every one of the 88 archived
real Classic decklists in our evaluation snapshot is exactly
constructible in the pool.

\subsection{Matchup fidelity and the agent-skill ceiling}
\label{sec:fidelity}
Validation asks: do simulated matchups between real decks reproduce
real matchup outcomes? Ground truth is the community-measured Classic
matchup matrix ($\sim$90k tracked games)~\cite{vicious_syndicate},
giving 20 in-scope archetype pairings with banded win rates and exact
ordinal ranks. Piloting both sides with a tuned 1-ply greedy agent
(27-feature linear evaluation) yields Spearman rank correlation 0.33
between simulated and real matchup orderings; piloting with guided
MCTS over the same evaluation yields 0.55--0.61. The improvement from
searching deeper with the \emph{same} evaluator --- concentrated in
exactly the archetype (Freeze Mage) whose real gameplan requires
valuing delayed wins --- establishes the central confound this paper
tracks throughout: \textbf{residual fidelity error is dominated by
agent skill, not card correctness}. A full-pool card audit that
changed game content (notably fixing token-summon triggers, without
which the very combo the 2014 nerf targeted did not exist in-engine)
moved aggregate fidelity by less than sampling noise, confirming the
attribution. A 82-deck round-robin against per-deck real win rates
gives Spearman 0.60.

\section{Historical Data and Ground Truth}
\label{sec:data}

\subsection{The deck archive and its date trap}
Ground truth derives from 33{,}697 ranked 2014 decklists (HearthPwn
archive~\cite{zenodo_dataset}), class-normalised to the simulator's
three-class world. One correction matters for anyone using this
archive: deck \emph{dates} are creation dates but card \emph{lists}
are last edits, so a deck created in June and edited in August can
contain Naxxramas cards ``before'' Naxxramas existed. Filtering by the
expansion tag of each deck's cards removed 3{,}725 such time-travelled
decks from the pre-Naxxramas window; without this filter the
prediction task is contaminated by its own answer key.

\subsection{Two shocks, and a dissociation}
\label{sec:shocks}
Table~\ref{tab:buzzard_series} shows the monthly series around the
September nerf. Two facts diverge instructively. At the \emph{card}
level, Starving Buzzard's within-Hunter adoption collapses from a
67.7\% pre-nerf mean to 16.9\% post-nerf, beginning in the nerf month
itself. At the \emph{class} level, Hunter's share of submitted decks
is flat to slightly rising (31.6\%$\to$33.0\%): the popular ``Hunter
died'' narrative is not supported --- players abandoned the card and
rebuilt the class. A simulator that reproduces the first is capturing
card-level power; one that also reproduces the second is capturing
metagame adaptation.

\subsection{Why shocks are the predictable part}
\label{sec:dynamics}
Before predicting shocks we tested the smoother alternative: does an
archetype's field-weighted simulated win rate (its matchup row
weighted by the current popularity mix) predict its next-month
popularity change, replicator-style? Over 36 (archetype, month) points
in the pre-Naxxramas window: Spearman $-0.004$ (and $0.081$ when the
matchup matrix is re-simulated with the stronger MCTS agent ---
the null is not an artifact of agent choice). The diagnosis is that
the target itself is noise at this granularity: at $\sim$580
classified decks/month the binomial standard error of a share change
($\sim$2.2$\pp$) is comparable to the observed mean absolute change
(2.9$\pp$), and the lag-1 autocorrelation of changes ($-0.46$) sits at
the $-0.5$ expected for constant-plus-noise. Static deck win rate and
play count are likewise uncorrelated in a separate 88-deck snapshot
(Spearman $-0.03$). Card adoption around shocks --- moves of
40--60$\pp$ against a $\sim$2$\pp$ noise floor --- is where the real
signal lives, and it is what the remainder of the paper predicts.

\section{Prediction Pipeline}
\label{sec:pipeline}

All experiments share the same skeleton: seed with the real pre-shock
decks, evolve under the post-shock card pool (expansion cards added,
or nerf patches applied), extract per-card adoption from the evolved
population, and compare against real post-shock adoption. Deck
evaluation is by simulated matches against a \emph{refreshing field}:
a gauntlet seeded with real pre-shock representatives of all three
classes, periodically re-sampled from the co-evolving populations'
elites with a fraction of real anchors retained (a fixed gauntlet
measurably overfits). Decks are piloted by the tuned greedy agent with
self-play-optimised weights. The no-change baseline --- predicting
that post-shock adoption equals pre-shock adoption --- is reported for
every metric.

\subsection{Attempt 1: unbiased evolution fails, with a mechanism}
Real pre-Naxxramas seeds evolving under the Classic+Naxxramas pool
adopted approximately 0\% of every card the real community adopted.
This is drift, not selection: a single-card swap changes a deck's win
rate by $\sim$1$\pp$, the per-deck evaluation budget (4--12 games
under early stopping) has a noise floor an order of magnitude above
that, and the observed adoption rate of new cards matches the pure
mutation-drift expectation. Whole-deck fitness evaluation cannot
resolve single-card quality at any budget we can afford ---
$\sim$37{,}000 games for the study as run.

\subsection{The marginal-value probe}
The probe isolates the card variable the way the evolutionary loop
cannot: for each candidate card and class, swap the candidate into a
fixed slot of five real host decks, play a fixed 60-game schedule per
host against a fixed nine-deck real field, and record the mean
win-rate delta versus the unmodified hosts. One number per
(card, class): the card's marginal value in realistic context.

\subsection{Attempt 2: probe-biased evolution}
Real players do not explore a new set uniformly: they read cards,
theorycraft, and disproportionately test promising ones. We model this
by biasing mutation --- probed-positive cards are proposed more often
and probed-negative slots replaced more often --- while keeping
selection untouched (24 fixed games per evaluation, 25 generations).
The claim tested is therefore explicitly \emph{``simulation-derived
card values plus evolutionary selection reproduce the shift''}, not
``blind evolution discovers it''; the unbiased failure is reported
alongside for exactly this reason.

\section{Results}
\label{sec:results}

\subsection{The probe recovers the real adoption ranking}
For the Naxxramas launch, the probe's top Hunter cards are precisely
the real community's staple package: Sludge Belcher $+6.5\pp$, Mad
Scientist $+5.3\pp$, Webspinner $+5.1\pp$, Haunted Creeper $+3.7\pp$
--- all cards that reached 45--74\% real adoption within weeks ---
while the cards the community never played probe at the bottom.
Spearman correlation between probed value and real pre-nerf adoption
is 0.35 (Hunter), 0.20 (Mage), 0.30 (Warrior) over the 22 new cards
per class. The two systematic misses are mechanically diagnostic:
Death's Bite (probed last for Warrior; 60\% real adoption) is a
weapon whose value lies in sequencing its deathrattle across turns,
and Duplicate ($-3.0\pp$ probed; 41\% real) is a secret whose value
is contextual card advantage --- both invisible to a 1-ply evaluator
piloting a single-card swap, and both instances of the agent-skill
ceiling measured independently in Sec.~\ref{sec:fidelity}.

\subsection{Expansion: predicting Naxxramas adoption}
Probe-biased evolution from real pre-Naxxramas seeds adopts the real
package. On the 38 Hunter cards whose real adoption moved by at least
5$\pp$ across the launch, the predicted direction matches reality for
30/38 (probe-biased) versus 16/38 (unbiased evolution) --- chance is
19/38. Magnitudes land in range for the package cards (e.g.\ Unstable
Ghoul predicted 45.8\% vs.\ real 44.9\%; Mad Scientist, Undertaker,
Sludge Belcher and Loatheb all rise from zero to real-order rates).
Death's Bite stays at 0\% predicted --- the probe's blind spot
propagates through the pipeline, as it must.

\subsection{Patch: predicting the nerf response}
The nerf experiment is the cleaner test (no new cards; the real
post-shock pool equals the simulated pool). Seeds are real
Naxxramas-era decks; the pool applies the real September patch.
Table~\ref{tab:nerf} shows the headline cards. The Buzzard collapse
is reproduced in direction and rough magnitude; nerfed Leeroy drops;
the un-nerfed Unleash the Hounds is correctly \emph{retained}; and
Webspinner's continued rise is called. The removal probe alone signs
the response: deleting Buzzard from patched-world Hunter decks gains
$+2.7\pp$, deleting Leeroy $+0.9\pp$ (Hunter) and $+1.6\pp$
(Warrior).

\subsection{The baseline, honestly}
The no-change baseline wins the \emph{overall} adoption Spearman
(0.62--0.96 vs.\ the prediction's 0.27--0.51): real decks are sticky,
and copying $\sim$100 unmoved cards exactly is unbeatable on a rank
metric dominated by non-movers. The prediction's win is specifically
on the movers --- direction hit-rate and the magnitudes above ---
which is the part of the question a no-change baseline cannot answer
by construction. We report both so the reader can weigh them.

\section{Learned Evaluators and Unseen Cards}
\label{sec:neural}

The probe's evaluator is a hand-designed linear function; new cards
were probed with full knowledge of their implementation. A stronger
test of generalisation replaces it with a learned value function that
has never seen the new cards. We encode each card by 68 ID-free
mechanical features (cost/stats, type, keywords, trigger and effect
categories, effect magnitudes) --- so a nerfed card encodes
differently from its original --- and train a small deep-sets value
network ($\sim$60k parameters) on pre-Naxxramas self-play only, with
Monte Carlo outcome targets and no search in the training loop.

Probing the Naxxramas cards with this net (zero Naxxramas exposure)
splits by mechanism support: Mage valuations \emph{beat} the linear
probe (Spearman vs.\ real adoption $+0.30$ vs.\ $+0.20$), Warrior
ties, and Hunter fails outright ($-0.075$ vs.\ $+0.35$) --- the
Hunter package is deathrattle-token cards, a mechanism with
essentially no support in pre-Naxxramas training data. Three
generations of fine-tuning with Naxxramas cards in the self-play pool
close the gap (Hunter $-0.075\to+0.34$; Mage rises to $+0.60$),
identifying mechanism-coverage of the training distribution, not
architecture, as the binding constraint. From the patch text alone,
the net signs both 2014 nerfs (removing nerfed Buzzard or nerfed
Leeroy improves its evaluation of the deck), agreeing with the linear
agent on Buzzard and improving on Leeroy. The two sequencing misses
(Death's Bite, Duplicate) persist under every static evaluator
tested --- they are properties of the policy, not the evaluation.

\section{Limitations}
\label{sec:limitations}

(a)~The probe bias injects simulation-derived knowledge into
exploration; unbiased evolution fails, and we frame the claim
accordingly. (b)~Both persistent misses trace to the piloting agent's
1-ply horizon, quantified independently by the fidelity study; claims
about sequencing-dependent cards are out of reach at this agent
strength. (c)~The engine models three classes and one era;
cross-class effects (e.g.\ a nerf shifting play toward an unmodelled
class) are invisible. (d)~The pre-nerf ground-truth window may retain
a small number of post-nerf-edited decks (the September patch
received no archive tag of its own); its healthy 73.4\% Buzzard rate
bounds the contamination as minor. (e)~Ground-truth matchup win rates
are banded ($\pm2.5\pp$); we use ordinal ranks where it matters.
(f)~Headline pipeline results are single runs; the probe aggregates
five hosts $\times$ 60 games per card, but replication across seeds
is future work for the evolutionary stages.

\section{Conclusion}

Given only pre-shock decklists and a validated simulator, a
marginal-value probe plus probe-biased evolutionary selection
predicts which cards a real player population adopted and abandoned
around both an expansion and a nerf --- with quantified failure modes
that localise to agent skill, an honest accounting of a strong
no-change baseline, and a learned-evaluator extension showing the
same predictions emerge from mechanics alone where training coverage
allows. We believe forward validation against measured human
adoption, rather than internal simulator consistency, is the standard
automated balancing should be held to, and this work provides a
first benchmark for it.
